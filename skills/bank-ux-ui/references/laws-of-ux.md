# Laws of UX (apply and cite by name)

Perception and grouping (Gestalt-based)
- **Law of Proximity**: items close together are perceived as related. Space groups; use more space between groups than within.
- **Law of Similarity**: similar look implies similar function. Make different actions look different.
- **Law of Common Region**: a border or background groups elements (cards, panels).
- **Law of Uniform Connectedness**: elements joined by lines/containers read as one unit.
- **Law of Prägnanz**: people read complex shapes in the simplest form. Prefer simple, regular layouts and icons.
- **Von Restorff (Isolation) Effect**: the different item is remembered. Use for one primary CTA only; if everything stands out, nothing does.
- **Aesthetic-Usability Effect**: attractive UIs are perceived as easier to use. Polish helps, but doesn't excuse poor usability.

Decision and cognition
- **Hick's Law**: decision time grows with number and complexity of choices. Reduce, group, progressively disclose, highlight a recommended option.
- **Choice Overload**: too many options reduce satisfaction and conversion.
- **Miller's Law / Working Memory**: people hold only a few items at once (~7±2 is a guide, not a hard limit). Chunk information; don't depend on memory across screens.
- **Chunking**: group content into meaningful units (phone numbers, card numbers, sections).
- **Cognitive Load**: remove extraneous load (clutter, unclear labels); keep intrinsic complexity manageable.
- **Occam's Razor**: of equal solutions, choose the simplest.
- **Tesler's Law (Conservation of Complexity)**: complexity can't be removed, only moved. Absorb it in the system (defaults, automation), not the user.
- **Selective Attention**: users filter out what looks like ads or noise. Put key info where users look, in content style.
- **Mental Model**: match what users already believe about how things work.
- **Jakob's Law**: users spend most time on other sites. Use familiar patterns (logo top-left links home, cart icon top-right, standard form controls).
- **Postel's Law**: be liberal in what you accept (flexible input formats), conservative in what you send.
- **Cognitive Bias**: design anticipates anchoring, defaults, social proof. Use ethically; no dark patterns.

Interaction and motor
- **Fitts's Law**: time to hit a target grows with distance and shrinks with size. Make primary targets large and near; place destructive ones away; use screen edges/corners on desktop; thumb zone on mobile. Min target sizes: see accessibility reference.
- **Doherty Threshold**: keep system response under ~400 ms for flow. Beyond that show feedback (spinner, skeleton, optimistic UI).
- **Flow**: balance challenge and ability, avoid interruptions, give clear goals and feedback.
- **Paradox of the Active User**: people skip manuals and start using. Teach in context (tooltips, empty states), not upfront tours.

Memory and motivation
- **Serial Position Effect**: first and last items are remembered best. Put key actions/nav items at start and end.
- **Peak-End Rule**: experiences are judged by their peak and ending. Design a good success/confirmation moment.
- **Zeigarnik Effect**: unfinished tasks are remembered. Progress indicators and saved drafts encourage completion.
- **Goal-Gradient Effect**: motivation rises near the goal. Show progress; pre-fill the first steps.
- **Parkinson's Law**: tasks expand to fill time. Short forms, sensible time limits, autofill.
- **Pareto Principle (80/20)**: a minority of features drive most use. Prioritize those in layout and QA.
